Volume & History
2 Four Decades of Growth

A Long Climb, a Sudden Fall,
and a Rapid Return

Australia's domestic aviation sector grew almost uninterrupted for nearly four decades. The Ansett collapse of 2001–02 caused a brief dip. COVID-19 was in a different category entirely — passenger numbers fell by more than 68% in a single year. The recovery since has been remarkable.

Source: BITRE Domestic Aviation Activity Annual Summary, 1984–2025

"In 2020, Australia went from 61 million domestic passengers to under 20 million in a single year — the steepest aviation collapse in the country's history."

Source: BITRE

The monthly heatmap reveals what the annual chart cannot: the rhythmic seasonality of Australian flying, peaking every July and October, interrupted by the pale void of 2020 and 2021. Load factors — how full planes actually were — cratered to as low as 28% in April 2020 as borders snapped shut overnight.

Airport Performance
4 Airports

Bigger Isn't Always
Better on Time

One of the most counterintuitive findings in Australian aviation data is that the largest airports are not the most punctual. Melbourne processes more domestic passengers than any other airport — yet its on-time departure rate sits below the national average. Adelaide, significantly smaller, punches well above its weight.

Source: BITRE OTP 2024

"Darwin recorded the lowest on-time departure rate of any major airport at just 59.9% — nearly 20 percentage points below Adelaide's 79.4%."

Source: BITRE OTP 2024

The grouped bar chart shows that small airports can consistently outperform large ones. South Australia's small airports — particularly Port Lincoln — achieve some of the highest punctuality in the country. The Northern Territory, by contrast, struggles regardless of airport size, with Darwin dragging down the territory's average considerably.

Punctuality
5 Punctuality

Every Airline Flew Less
Punctually in 2024

Comparing 2019 pre-pandemic figures to 2024 data tells a sobering story for every carrier. Not a single major domestic airline matched its 2019 on-time departure record. The reasons include post-COVID staffing challenges, airspace congestion from recovering international traffic, and higher overall load factors compressing turnaround times.

Worst Routes
6 Worst-Performing Routes

Regional and Leisure Routes
Carry the Most Delays

The 20 worst-performing domestic routes in 2024 share a common profile: they are long-haul connections to leisure or regional destinations, often dependent on a single airline and disproportionately affected when aircraft are delayed elsewhere in the network. Darwin features four times in the bottom 20.

Source: BITRE OTP 2024

"Hamilton Island–Sydney had the worst on-time rate in the country at just 55% — meaning nearly half of all departures were late."

Routes connecting leisure destinations like Hamilton Island, Proserpine, and Broome consistently rank among the worst-performing. These routes often rely on aircraft rotated from other services, meaning a disruption anywhere in the chain cascades into delays. Darwin's geographic isolation and challenging operating conditions compound this effect.

Load Factor
7 Load Factors

How Full Are
Australia's Planes?

A load factor compares how many passengers actually flew against how many seats were available. Routes closer to the diagonal line are flying at or near capacity. Across almost all routes, 2024 load factors were significantly higher than pre-pandemic years — a sign of lean scheduling and robust demand.

Source: BITRE Domestic Activity 2024

Regional and leisure routes like Broome–Perth and Hamilton Island–Melbourne are flying at nearly 89% capacity — airlines have tightly matched supply to demand on these thinner routes. Major city-pair routes cluster in the 80–87% range, leaving little slack for disruptions.

Recovery
8 Recovery

Which Routes Bounced Back
— and Which Did Not?

Comparing 2024 passenger volumes to 2019 reveals that recovery has been highly uneven. Some regional mining and resource routes — particularly in Western Australia — have far exceeded their pre-pandemic volumes, driven by the resources boom. Business travel corridors, meanwhile, have struggled to return to 2019 levels as hybrid work reduces corporate flying.

Summary
✈ Key Takeaways

What the Data Tells Us

The East Dominates
The Sydney–Melbourne–Brisbane triangle accounts for the overwhelming majority of domestic passenger traffic. Geography and population concentration make this corridor irreplaceable.
COVID Changed Everything
The pandemic dealt Australian aviation its worst shock in history. Recovery has been near-complete in volume, but punctuality has not recovered — and some routes remain permanently disrupted.
Size ≠ Quality
Bigger airports and larger airlines do not automatically deliver better on-time performance. Operational complexity, network dependencies, and geographic factors play an equal or greater role.
Resources Drive Recovery
Western Australia's resource sector has created a new tier of domestic aviation demand, pushing several WA regional routes well above their 2019 passenger counts.
